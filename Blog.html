*Heroes of Metro Magic*

Week 1

I really enjoyed both writings and felt both a connection between them and to myself. Some of the concepts I parsed in each writing run parallel to the bigger ideas I will try to implement in my Visual Magic Project (Titled Heroes of Metro Magic... more on that later). Starting with In Defense of the Poor Image: As a rapidly aging Millennial that would like to believe I am nuanced with refined taste in media, reading this article made me realize that my earliest inspirations to make a livelihood of working in the field started through the consumption of the Poor Image; from recording Saturday morning cartoons recorded on old VHS tapes (with artifacts of the previous episodes still tracking through), to downloading pirated videos on Kazaa that usually contained surprise edits from whoever uploaded them, and playing old Flash games on websites banned on school browsers that most certainly only exist on InternetArchive. Without really knowing the concept, I am largely a product of Poor Images.

There’s a sense of longing that comes with that. Living in a post-internet era, the joy I found in consuming physical media and Web 1.0 growing up is hard to come by today. So rare are the simple experiences that come with being unconnected, because you simply can’t consume media without it being shared over a global-sized table. There are strengths to being as connected as we are today. Still, there are also vulnerabilities and threats to exposure, and strangely, reading An Account of Peach Blossom Spring conjured many of those feelings, despite being written roughly 1,500 years before the internet. Reading about the fisherman stumbling upon a village cut off from the rest of the world, maintaining a simpler way of life, felt like stumbling upon an old GeoCities site, totally intact from the doom-scrolling dread of the present.

It’s this sense of hauntology, the hunting of cultural memory, that I want to explore deeper in my project. While the direction and execution of it may change over the course of this class, I want to explore the search for joy in simple things amongst the chaos of the now; like a form of arcane magic long forgotten amongst the complexity of our current landscape, but still able to alchemize dark times into the most important gold: Joy.

Week 2

Working in media for the last decade and a half, I found it particularly haunting to read The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction, in that even today, the media we consume changes so rapidly, and with it, the meaning and intended impact that it carries. This not only affects the consumer, but the creator and their livelihoods as well. More on this in a moment, but first I want to reflect on the concept of “Aura”. Reading Benjamin’s definition of aura took me back to an exhibition at the Met last year; I love the work of Caspar David Friedrich, specifically his usage of Rückenfigur, so when the museum announced Caspar David Friedrich: The Soul of Nature, I had to go. One particular painting hit me hard: Monk By the Sea. The sheer size and scale of the painting is overwhelming. The ominous and dark clouds are so overpoweringly large, not just to the small monk in the painting, but to the viewer as well, that you almost feel like the monk gazing out into the abyss. I was so transported by the painting, I had to purchase the art book in the gift shop. However, to this day, I almost regret it. Looking at the small printout of the photo, wedged between paragraphs of text and other photos, is so painfully depressing compared to viewing the painting in the moment as it was intended that I feel embarrassed for anyone who purchased the mass-produced book that hasn’t seen the actual photo. It loses all of its impact and grandeur.

Benjamin does a great job with this concept in illustrating Marshall McLuhan’s work several decades earlier. When he further details the changes film and photography have brought on the art and media landscape, I couldn’t help but realize that his thoughts could easily be translated to today. When he quotes Georges Duhamel’s disdain for motion pictures, my mind wandered to what Duhamel might think of today’s TikTok-forward consumption of media, where 9x16 formats reign supreme, and anything over 30 seconds has lost the attention of its audience. As someone who grew up in the appointment television era of media, it pains me to see that format dying, being replaced with bingeable streaming options and a proclivity for bite-size, phone-friendly formats that people consume at their own pace and time. There is almost zero aura to that.

A brief, final observation from a quote that also deeply struck me: “The distinction between author and public is about to lose its basic character. The difference becomes merely functional; it may vary from case to case. At any moment the reader is ready to turn into a writer”. This passage could not be any more real than it was at the time of this writing. Now, anyone can be a photographer, filmmaker, writer, reporter, or actor; everyone has a 4k camera in their pocket, with AI color-balance and focus tools that can stream instantly to billions of people around the world over the internet. Anyone can code a blog and write their thoughts down for anyone to read, as I am doing now, and anyone can get in front the aforementioned camera, or mic, or live stream, and report or perform for the masses to their liking. It’s surreal the weight that quote holds to this day.

Week 3

Reading As We May Think was equal parts terrifying and enlightening for me. Seeing the world of technology through Bush’s eyes in the 40s, one full of hopeful admiration for where the world of science was heading on the heels of World War II, was, in a sense, endearing. It was also so interesting to see all his predictions come true in some fashion; the cyclops camera is eerily similar in concept to Meta Glasses. The concept of Microfilm is essentially what the internet has become, with entire volumes of knowledge readily available to anyone. Between the Vocoder and Thinking Machine, you can draw parallels to what we would know as an AI Agent, whom we can speak to and work with. And finally, I couldn’t help but think of algorithmic AI when Bush defined the Memex, which feeds us suggestions and closes our mental loop by associating our thoughts.

The terrifying part is that, as these premonitions have come to fruition, they are not as idyllic as Bush romanticized in his essay. Meta Glasses have become such an icon of intrusiveness that they’re almost synonymous with voyeurism. The internet is arguably the most impactful invention of all time, and has changed our direction as a species forever. Still, with the advent of Web 2.0 and the amount of misinformation running rampant, it is a lot less like the Encyclopedia Britannica and more like a town square where anyone can get on a soapbox and scream their thoughts to the masses as fact. And of course, AI, both AI agents and the algorithm, have so many problems that it’s hard to keep track of. When I read this article, I thought of the 1939 and 1964 World’s Fairs in Flushing, which are extensively archived at the Queens Museum. Last time I was there, I felt so much hope for the future, with the world coming together to showcase its latest technologies to build a better tomorrow. However, while many of those developments came true, you get the feeling that many of these advances in energy and technology ultimately lead to more problems (IBM’s computers, the Department of Defense missile technology, DuPont’s plastics, etc.). When you walk out of the museum and see the dilapidated viewing towers and Tent of Tomorrow, it’s almost too perfect a metaphor that certain ideas don’t age gracefully.
